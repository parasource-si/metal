bots are only as good as what you hand them. none of this is required, but people who get the most out of Metal do most of it.

## 1. one bot, one job

a researcher, an inbox helper, a bookkeeper. a bot with one job gets better at it, builds the right memory, and is easy to check on. a bot that does everything does nothing well.

| bot | its job |
| --- | --- |
| researcher | reads the 40 tabs so you don't. comes back with sources and a straight answer. |
| inbox helper | reads what came in overnight, drafts replies in your voice, puts what needs you on top. |
| bookkeeper | matches receipts to charges and tells you when a subscription got pricier. |
| social writer | drafts the week's posts from what you shipped. posts nothing until you say so. |

start with one. hire the second when the first is earning its keep.

## 2. write the job like a note to a new hire

say what it does, what "done" looks like, and what it must never do.

<div class="say"><p><small>vague</small>"help me with email."</p><p><small>clear</small>"every morning, read my inbox. draft replies to anything from clients, in my voice, short. put what needs me at the top. never send anything yourself."</p></div>

## 3. give your first message a goal, the context and a shape

the bot can't read your mind. three things make almost any request land: what you want, what it needs to know, and how you want the answer.

<div class="say"><p><small>vague</small>"look into competitors."</p><p><small>clear</small>"find the five closest competitors to our invoicing app. for each: price, who it's for, one thing they do better than us. a table, then two lines on what we should copy."</p></div>

## 4. hand it the thing, not a description of it

drop the pdf, the screenshot, the spreadsheet straight into the chat. a bot reading the real file beats a bot guessing from your summary. see [files and apps](/docs/files-and-apps).

## 5. steer, don't start over

you don't have to wait for a bot to finish. send another message while it works and it adjusts. heading the wrong way? press [[⌘ .]] to stop it, say what you meant, and carry on in the same chat so it keeps what it already learned.

## 6. let it ask at first, then loosen up

keep a new bot on **ask for approval** for a few days, so it checks with you before running commands, changing files or sending anything. read the cards, and once you trust a bot with a kind of task, allow it. see [a bot's computer](/docs/computer#asking-first).

## 7. tell it once, and it remembers

"i only fly aisle." "dana signs off on anything over €500." a bot writes things like that into its [memory](/docs/memory) and uses them next month. things every bot should know about you (your name, time zone, how you like to be written to) go in **about me** in settings.

## 8. if you ask twice, make it a routine

anything you find yourself asking again is a routine waiting to happen. just say it:

<div class="say"><p><small>once</small>"what's on my calendar tomorrow?"</p><p><small>routine</small>"every weekday at 7, tell me what's on my calendar and flag anything that clashes."</p></div>

more in [routines](/docs/routines).

## 9. when work passes between jobs, use a group chat

put the researcher, the writer and the reviewer in one [group chat](/docs/group-chats) and they hand work to each other with @mentions. you step in when one of them needs a yes.

## 10. check the first few runs

read what a new bot or routine does the first few times, and correct it right in the chat ("shorter", "skip newsletters", "always include the link"). it learns fast, and after a week you'll only glance.

## 11. move to dusk cloud when bots become part of your day

if a routine missed because your laptop was asleep, or you want to text a bot from your phone at night, that's the sign. [dusk cloud](/docs/dusk-cloud) keeps your bots always available, with nothing to look after.
